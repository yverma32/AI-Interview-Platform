Most candidates can write a working solution. They fall apart when the interviewer says, "Okay, what's the time complexity, and why?" That pause — the one where you stare at your own code like you've never seen it before — is what separates offers from rejections at Razorpay, Google India, and even TCS Smart Hiring rounds. Time complexity interview questions are not really about Big-O theory. They're about whether you can think out loud under pressure and defend your reasoning to a skeptical engineer sitting across from you.

This post is about exactly that: how to state complexity, how to explain it without sounding like you're reciting a textbook, and how to hold your ground when the interviewer pushes back.

## The interviewer isn't checking if you know O(n log n)

They already assume you know the basics. What they're actually checking is whether you can reason about your own code in real time. When a Swiggy engineer asks "what's the complexity of your approach?", they want to hear you trace through the logic, not recite a memorized label.

The difference sounds like this. Wrong: "It's O(n log n) because I'm sorting." Right: "The sort takes O(n log n). After that I do a single pass, which is O(n). So overall it's O(n log n), dominated by the sort." One sentence of justification changes the entire impression. It tells the interviewer you actually understand what's happening in your code.

## Start with the structure, not the label

Before you say a single Big-O term, walk through what your code is doing structurally. How many loops? Are any of them nested? Are you calling a library function that has its own cost?

A good habit: point at each block of code and narrate it. "This outer loop runs n times. Inside it, I'm doing a hash map lookup, which is O(1) average. So the whole thing is O(n)." That narration is what interviewers at Microsoft India and Amazon India train their interviewers to listen for. They want to hear you decompose the problem, not just announce a result.

If you skip straight to the label, you've given the interviewer nothing to probe. If you walk through the structure, you've shown your reasoning and made the follow-up conversation much easier.

## Nested loops are not always O(n²) — say that out loud

This is where candidates lose points they shouldn't. You see two loops and immediately say O(n²). The interviewer frowns. You second-guess yourself. The spiral begins.

Two nested loops are O(n²) only when both iterate over the same input of size n. If the inner loop runs over a fixed-size structure (say, 26 characters in an alphabet frequency array), the inner loop is O(1) and the whole thing is O(n). If the outer loop runs over n elements and the inner loop runs over the remaining elements, it's O(n²). If the inner loop is a binary search over a sorted subarray, it's O(n log n).

Say this reasoning out loud. "The inner loop looks like it could be O(n), but it's iterating over a fixed set of 26 characters, so it's constant. Total complexity: O(n)." That one sentence saves you from the most common trap in live coding rounds.

## Space complexity gets asked second — have the answer ready

Almost every interviewer who asks about time complexity will follow up with space complexity. Candidates who weren't thinking about it stumble. Candidates who were thinking about it from the start sound sharp.

As you write your solution, keep a mental note of what you're allocating. A hash map that grows with input? That's O(n) space. A recursion stack that goes n levels deep? Also O(n). An in-place sort like heapsort? O(1) auxiliary space, though the sort itself is O(n log n) time.

When the follow-up comes, you want to say: "I'm using a hash map to store frequencies, so space is O(n) in the worst case where all characters are unique." Specific. Justified. Done.

## How to handle the "can you do better?" follow-up

This is the most common follow-up to time complexity interview questions, and it's the one that causes the most visible panic. The interviewer asks if you can improve the complexity. You don't know the answer. What do you do?

First, don't guess randomly. Saying "maybe O(log n)?" without reasoning is worse than saying nothing. Instead, think out loud about the lower bound. "The problem requires reading every element at least once, so we can't do better than O(n) for time. My current solution is already O(n), so I don't think we can improve the time complexity further." That's a complete, defensible answer.

If you genuinely think there's a better approach but can't see it, say so: "I think there might be a smarter data structure here, but I'd need a moment to think through it." Interviewers at Flipkart and PhonePe have told us they'd rather hear honest reasoning than a confident wrong answer.

## The amortized case trips up even strong candidates

Dynamic arrays (like Python lists or Java ArrayLists) have O(1) amortized append. Candidates who say "O(n) because of resizing" are technically right about the worst case but wrong about the practical complexity. Interviewers at product companies specifically probe this.

When you're working with a structure that has amortized behavior, name it. "Appending to a dynamic array is O(1) amortized. Occasionally it resizes, which is O(n), but averaged across all operations the cost per append is constant." If you don't mention amortized analysis, a sharp interviewer will ask, and you'll have to explain it under pressure anyway. Better to bring it up yourself.

## Practice stating complexity before you finish the code

Most candidates figure out the complexity after they've written the full solution. That's too late in a live interview. By then you're under time pressure, you're mentally tired, and you have to reverse-engineer your own code.

A better habit: as you write each block of code, say its complexity out loud. "I'm going to use a heap here, so insertion will be O(log k)." This does two things. It forces you to think about efficiency as you code, which often leads to better solutions. And it means that when the interviewer asks, you already have the full picture assembled.

Do two timed coding sessions back-to-back when you practice. In the second session, you'll notice exactly which parts of your complexity reasoning you were faking in the first one.

## What "O(n) on average" actually means — and when to say it

Hash map operations are O(1) average, O(n) worst case. Quicksort is O(n log n) average, O(n²) worst case. Many candidates say "O(1)" for hash lookups and get challenged. Many say "O(n log n)" for quicksort and get challenged.

The correct phrasing: "O(1) average case, assuming a good hash function and low collision rate. Worst case is O(n) with degenerate input." That qualification tells the interviewer you understand the assumptions behind the label, which is the actual point of the question.

For interviews at Google India or Amazon India, expect to be pushed on this. For TCS or Infosys technical rounds, average-case answers are usually accepted without challenge. Know your audience, but always be ready to go deeper.

## Defending your answer when the interviewer disagrees

Sometimes the interviewer says "I think that's actually O(n²)" and they're wrong. Sometimes they're right and you made an error. Either way, the worst response is to immediately cave.

Walk back through your reasoning. "Let me trace through it again. The outer loop runs n times. The inner operation is a hash lookup, which is O(1). So I'm still getting O(n). Can you point to where you see the quadratic behavior?" That's not combative. It's collaborative. It shows you can hold a technical position while remaining open to being corrected.

If you trace through and realize you were wrong, say so clearly: "You're right, I missed that the inner call is itself O(n). So the total is O(n²). Thanks for catching that." Interviewers remember candidates who correct themselves gracefully far more than candidates who never make mistakes.
