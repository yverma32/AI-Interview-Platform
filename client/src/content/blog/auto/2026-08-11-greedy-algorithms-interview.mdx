Most candidates who fail a greedy algorithms interview question don't fail because they forgot the algorithm. They fail because they picked greedy when the problem required dynamic programming, coded it up confidently, and only realized the mistake when the interviewer said "does that work for this case?" and showed them a counterexample. By then, fifteen minutes are gone.

This post is about one specific skill: how to quickly verify that a greedy approach is actually correct before you commit to it. It's not a list of greedy problems to memorize. It's a thinking process you can run in about ninety seconds at a whiteboard.

## Why Greedy Feels Right Even When It's Wrong

Greedy algorithms feel intuitive. You make the locally optimal choice at each step and trust that it leads to a global optimum. For some problems, that's exactly right. For others, the local optimum at step three destroys your chance of reaching the global optimum by step ten.

The trap is that greedy solutions are almost always simpler to code than DP solutions. So when you're under pressure in a Flipkart or Amazon interview, your brain pushes you toward greedy because it looks like the path of least resistance. That instinct will betray you on problems like 0/1 Knapsack, where the greedy-by-density approach fails the moment item weights don't divide cleanly.

Recognizing the trap early is the entire game.

## The Two Questions That Separate Greedy from DP

Before you write a single line of code, ask yourself two questions about the problem.

First: does the optimal solution for the full problem always contain the optimal solution for each subproblem? If yes, you have optimal substructure, which is required for both greedy and DP. If no, neither applies cleanly and you need to think harder.

Second: does making the locally best choice now ever close off a globally better option later? If yes, greedy will fail. If no, greedy is likely safe.

The second question is the one most candidates skip. They confirm optimal substructure and jump straight to coding. Spend thirty seconds on the second question. It will save you from the counterexample ambush.

## The Counterexample Test You Should Run at the Board

Once you have a candidate greedy strategy, try to break it yourself before the interviewer does. This is not pessimism; it's the move that signals seniority.

Construct an input where the greedy choice at the first step is obvious, then ask: does following that choice force me into a suboptimal state later? For the classic Activity Selection problem, picking the earliest-finishing activity first is safe because finishing early never blocks more future activities than any other choice. You can verify this in two or three small examples.

For Coin Change with arbitrary denominations (say, coins of 1, 3, and 4, making change for 6), greedy-by-largest-coin gives you 4+1+1 = three coins. DP gives you 3+3 = two coins. One small example, thirty seconds, and you've caught the failure.

If you can't find a counterexample after two or three targeted attempts, that's not proof, but it's a reasonable signal to proceed with greedy while staying alert.

## The Exchange Argument: Your Actual Proof Tool

When you need more than a gut check, use the exchange argument. It's the standard technique for proving greedy correctness and interviewers at Google and Microsoft are familiar with it. Knowing it by name earns you points.

The argument goes like this: assume there exists an optimal solution that differs from your greedy solution. Find the first point where they diverge. Show that you can swap the optimal solution's choice at that point for the greedy choice without making the solution worse. If you can always make that swap, the greedy solution is at least as good as any optimal solution, so it is optimal.

You don't need to write a formal proof at a whiteboard. You need to say: "I'm going to assume an optimal solution makes a different choice here, and show that swapping to my greedy choice can't hurt." Then walk through it with the specific problem in front of you. That's enough to demonstrate rigorous thinking.

## Greedy Algorithms Interview Mistakes That Cost Offers

The patterns that trip up candidates in greedy algorithms interview rounds at product companies are remarkably consistent. Here are the three most common.

Applying fractional knapsack logic to 0/1 knapsack. Fractional knapsack is solvable greedily by value-per-weight ratio. 0/1 knapsack is not, because you can't take fractions of items. The problems look similar. They are not the same.

Assuming sorted input means greedy works. Sorting is often a step inside a greedy solution, but sorted input doesn't guarantee that greedy applies. The problem structure has to justify it, not the input format.

Confusing "greedy works on examples" with "greedy is correct." Three passing examples don't prove correctness. They raise your confidence. Use the exchange argument or a known theorem (like the matroid intersection theorem for advanced cases) if you want to actually prove it.

## When Greedy Is Provably the Right Tool

Some problem families are reliably solvable with greedy, and knowing them cold saves you the verification overhead.

Interval scheduling (maximize number of non-overlapping intervals): always sort by end time, always pick earliest-finishing. This is provably optimal and the exchange argument is textbook.

Huffman encoding: always merge the two lowest-frequency nodes. Provably optimal by a standard greedy proof.

Minimum spanning trees via Kruskal's or Prim's: both are greedy and both are provably correct by the cut property of MSTs.

Single-source shortest paths in graphs with non-negative edge weights via Dijkstra's: greedy by minimum tentative distance, provably correct.

If your problem maps cleanly onto one of these families, you can skip the full verification and just implement. If it doesn't map cleanly, do the verification.

## How to Communicate Your Reasoning to the Interviewer

Interviewers at Razorpay, Swiggy, and similar companies aren't just checking whether you got the right algorithm. They're watching how you think. A candidate who says "I'll try greedy here because making the locally optimal choice seems safe, let me check that with a quick counterexample attempt" looks far stronger than one who silently codes a greedy solution and hopes it's right.

Say your reasoning out loud. Name the approach. Attempt to break it. If you find a counterexample, pivot to DP and explain why. If you don't find one, say so and proceed. This narration turns a potential failure into a demonstration of engineering judgment.

One concrete habit: before coding, state your greedy invariant explicitly. "At every step, I'm picking the interval that ends earliest. My claim is that this choice never blocks more future intervals than any other choice I could make." Stating the invariant forces you to think it through and gives the interviewer something concrete to engage with.

## Practice Greedy and DP Back-to-Back, Not Separately

The best way to build the instinct for when greedy applies is to solve the same problem both ways whenever possible. Take Coin Change. Solve it greedily with standard denominations (1, 5, 10, 25) where greedy works. Then solve it with arbitrary denominations where greedy fails and DP is required. Sit with both solutions at the same time and ask what structural property of the first problem made greedy valid.

Do this with five or six problem pairs and the pattern becomes visceral, not just intellectual. You'll start feeling the difference between problems where local choices are independent and problems where they interact across the timeline.

When an interviewer at a product company gives you a greedy problem, the question they're actually asking is not "do you know greedy algorithms?" They're asking "do you know when greedy is valid and why?" Any candidate who has done fifty LeetCode greedy problems knows the algorithm. Very few can explain why it works.
