Most candidates pick a side. Either they write everything recursively because it reads cleanly, or they avoid recursion entirely because they once hit a stack overflow and never recovered emotionally. Neither habit serves you well. The recursion vs iteration interview question isn't really about which one is correct. It's about whether you understand both well enough to switch on demand.

Interviewers at Razorpay, Google India, and Amazon will ask you to implement a solution one way, watch you finish, and then say: "Now do it the other way." If you can't, the signal is clear — you memorized a pattern, you didn't understand the problem.

Here's how to build that flexibility before your next round.

## Why interviewers ask you to convert between the two

The conversion request is a probe for depth, not a trick. When an interviewer at Flipkart or Microsoft asks you to rewrite your recursive tree traversal iteratively, they want to see three things: whether you understand call stack mechanics, whether you can reason about space complexity changes, and whether you stay calm when the problem shifts under you.

Candidates who only know one form tend to panic. They'll say "I think you can do it iteratively but I'm not sure how to start." That's a yellow flag. Candidates who can walk through the conversion calmly, explaining trade-offs as they go, look senior regardless of their years of experience.

The other reason interviewers push for the iterative version specifically: production code at most Indian product companies runs on JVM or Node runtimes where deep call stacks are a real operational concern. Knowing that your recursive DFS will blow up on a tree with 100,000 nodes matters. Knowing how to fix it matters more.

## Recursion is a call stack. Once you see that, conversion is mechanical.

This is the mental model that unlocks everything. Every recursive function is secretly using the call stack to store state between calls. The base case is the termination condition. The recursive call is the loop iteration. The return value bubbles up through the stack frames.

When you convert to iteration, you're not doing something fundamentally different. You're making the stack explicit. You create a stack data structure, push your initial state, and loop until the stack is empty. The logic inside the loop mirrors the logic inside the recursive function almost line for line.

Once you internalize this, the conversion stops feeling like a new problem. It's the same problem with a different storage mechanism.

## The three-step conversion method that works under pressure

You don't have time to reason from first principles during a live interview. Use this three-step method:

**Step 1: Identify what each recursive call "remembers."** These are your stack frames. In a tree traversal, each call remembers the current node. In a path-finding problem, each call might remember the current node plus the path taken so far.

**Step 2: Push that state onto an explicit stack at the start of the loop.** Initialize the stack with your starting state before the loop begins.

**Step 3: Replace the recursive call with a push.** Every place you would have called the function recursively, push the new state onto the stack instead. Replace the base case with a continue or break condition at the top of the loop.

That's it. Walk the interviewer through these three steps out loud. Even if your syntax is slightly off, narrating the method correctly earns you significant credit.

## Factorial and Fibonacci: where most candidates get tripped up first

These two problems are where the recursion vs iteration interview question usually starts, and they're where candidates reveal shaky fundamentals.

Factorial is the easier conversion. The recursive version computes `n * factorial(n-1)`. The iterative version is a for loop multiplying a running product. The key insight to say out loud: the recursive version has O(n) space because of call stack depth, the iterative version has O(1) space. That one sentence is worth points.

Fibonacci is trickier because naive recursion is O(2^n) time. The iterative version with two variables is O(n) time and O(1) space. If you mention memoization as a middle path (keeping recursion but caching results), you're showing you know more than the binary choice. Interviewers at CRED and PhonePe specifically like when candidates identify that memoized recursion and bottom-up iteration are solving the same subproblem structure differently.

## Tree traversals: when the iterative version is genuinely harder to write

Binary tree traversal is where the conversion gets non-trivial. In-order traversal recursively is five lines. Iteratively, you need to handle the "go left as far as possible, then process, then go right" logic explicitly, which requires tracking a current pointer separate from the stack.

Practice this one until it's automatic. The iterative in-order traversal comes up at Amazon India SDE-1 and SDE-2 rounds regularly. The pattern: push nodes as you go left, pop when you can't go further left, process the node, then move to its right child.

Pre-order is easier iteratively (push right child first, then left, process on pop). Post-order iteratively is the hardest and worth knowing if you're targeting senior roles, because it requires either two stacks or a reverse trick.

## Graph DFS and BFS: the one place iteration is almost always expected

For graph problems, experienced interviewers expect the iterative BFS by default. Recursive DFS on a graph with 10,000 nodes will hit Python's default recursion limit of 1,000 and crash. In a real system, that's a production incident.

When you're doing graph problems, write BFS with an explicit queue from the start. If you write recursive DFS, immediately note the stack overflow risk and offer to convert. At companies like Swiggy or Zepto where backend scale is a live concern, showing that awareness is a differentiator.

The rule of thumb worth memorizing: use a queue for BFS, use an explicit stack for iterative DFS, and reserve recursive DFS for tree problems where depth is bounded and you're confident about it.

## When recursion is the right answer and you should defend it

Iterative isn't always better. For divide-and-conquer algorithms like merge sort and quicksort, the recursive structure mirrors the problem structure so cleanly that forcing an iterative version makes the code harder to read and harder to verify correct. Same for backtracking problems like N-Queens or generating permutations.

If an interviewer asks you to convert a backtracking solution to iterative form, it's fair to push back briefly: "I can do that, but the iterative version with an explicit stack will be significantly harder to reason about. The recursive version's call stack is doing real work here, not just accumulating depth. Want me to proceed with the conversion or would it be more useful to discuss the space trade-offs?" That's a senior-engineer response. It shows judgment, not avoidance.

## The space complexity answer interviewers want to hear

Every time you convert between forms, the interviewer is waiting for you to address space complexity. Say it before they ask.

Recursive: O(h) space where h is the depth of the recursion (height of the tree, length of the call chain). In the worst case for an unbalanced tree, that's O(n).

Iterative with explicit stack: also O(h) in the worst case for DFS. You haven't saved space by converting. You've just made the stack visible and controllable.

BFS with a queue: O(w) where w is the maximum width of the tree. For a balanced binary tree, that's O(n/2), which is still O(n). For problems where the tree is wide but shallow, BFS uses more space than DFS.

Knowing these numbers and saying them unprompted signals that you're thinking about production, not just about getting the test cases to pass.

## Build the conversion reflex before your interview, not during it

The candidates who handle the "now do it the other way" moment well aren't smarter. They've just done the conversion enough times that it's a reflex. Two weeks before your interview, take every tree and graph problem you've solved recursively and rewrite it iteratively. Then take three iterative solutions and convert them back. Do this with a timer. The goal is to get the conversion time under ten minutes for standard problems.

Pair that with mock interview rounds where someone can actually ask the follow-up question out loud. Reading about conversion is one thing. Narrating it under time pressure while someone watches is different.
